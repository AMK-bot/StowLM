# Bad load plans drain about $2.4 billion yearly

Bad container-ship load plans cost the world roughly **$0.35 billion (low), $2.4 billion (mid) and $6.6 billion (high) a year**, which is about **$53,000, $356,000 and $987,000 per ship per year** across the 6,642-ship cellular fleet. Every one of those dollars is a **projection**. No carrier, terminal, insurer or regulator publishes the share of any loss that the load plan causes, so each line is a measured world baseline multiplied by an assumed load-plan share. The baselines are measured and large: a **$31.5–43 billion** container-fleet fuel bill, an average port call of **40.5 hours**, about **1,300 containers lost overboard** and roughly **one cargo-fire death a year**. Berth delay is the largest line, at **about $1.1 billion at mid, or about 100 berth hours per ship per year**. It is followed by avoidable restow fees (**$0.42 billion**), trim and ballast fuel (**$0.39 billion**) and slot revenue at risk (**$0.36 billion**, gross revenue that mostly moves to the next sailing rather than vanishing). Safety is small in dollars (**about $65 million at mid**) and in lives (**about 0.7 deaths a year attributed, range 0.03–5.6**). Investigators find that stowage mostly shapes how bad a fire or stack collapse gets, while misdeclared cargo and weather decide whether one happens. The weakest input in the project's own estimate is its **5/10/15 % restow share**. The only port-wide measured restow rate, at the transhipment hub of Colombo, is **1.35–2.9 % of throughput for all restows**. The project's *low* case for avoidable restows therefore already sits at the top of the measured range for *total* restows, and its mid case is **about 12 times** the evidence-based avoidable figure of roughly 0.9 % of moves. Correcting that one input cuts the **$7.0 billion** mid pool in [`docs/impact.md`](../docs/impact.md) to about **$2.3 billion**.

## Four categories sum to $0.35, $2.4 and $6.6 billion a year, all of it projection

Each line multiplies a measured baseline by a share that is attributed to the load plan, and that share is always an assumption. The roll-up keeps one resource in one line. The restow fee is the price the carrier pays for the extra crane work, so crane work is counted once, inside the fee. Delay values only the ship's own time (charter hire), which is a different resource from the terminal crane gang the fee pays for. The fuel line covers only sea-passage fuel lost to poor trim and corrective ballast. Shut-out charges are not added. Most roll-overs are driven by bookings, not by plan errors, and the shut-outs a plan does cause (weight- or stability-limited departures) sit in the slot line. The table divides the world totals by the 6,642 fully cellular ships Alphaliner counted in November 2025 ([AXS Marine](https://public.axsmarine.com/blog/key-container-shipping-data-trends-november-2025)). A broader count of 7,497 ships would lower every per-ship figure by about 11 % ([Container News](https://container-news.com/containership-orderbook-reaches-record-11-93-million-teu/)).

The high column uses a bounded delay case. The researcher's original high delay of **$7.09 billion** fails two measured ceilings (see the delay section), and keeping it would raise the high total to **$11.4 billion**. Two overlaps remain and both are named rather than hidden. The first is fuel against slots. In a 1986 simulation, a better container distribution either let up to 14 more boxes ride without ballast or cut ballast to save 5–10 t of fuel per voyage ([Geissler, via TRID](https://trid.trb.org/View/429198)). One improvement buys one or the other, so adding both overstates the high case. The second is safety: boxes lost to fires also appear in the overboard counts. Two costs are counted nowhere, which keeps the totals conservative. One is the fuel burned speeding up to make up a late departure, which CPPI flags as the usual consequence of an unplanned port hour ([CPPI 2023](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099060324114539683/pdf/P17583313892300871be641a5ea7b90e0e6.pdf)). The other is the downstream inventory cost of late cargo.

## Restow fees cost about $0.4 billion, built on one port's measured rate

Only one port-wide restow statistic turned up, and it is lower than most of the figures quoted in the trade. Sri Lanka Ports Authority reports **83,935 TEU restowed out of 6,209,068 TEU handled in 2017 (1.35 %)** and **101,879 out of 7,047,486 in 2018 (1.45 %)**. By terminal, the 2018 shares were 0.42 % at the SLPA terminals, 2.06 % at SAGT and 1.86 % at CICT ([SLPA Annual Report 2018](https://www.parliament.lk/uploads/documents/paperspresented/annual-report-srilanka-ports-authority-2018.pdf)). In 2022 Colombo restowed **196,269 TEU of 6.86 million (2.86 %)** ([The Morning](https://www.themorning.lk/articles/sBZ9a4XsIt2nHTa9Z1xm)). SLPA does not say whether it counts a restowed box once or once per lift. SMDG's terminal-performance standard counts a restow through the quay as two moves, one off and one on, though that point was checked only from a search extract ([SMDG TPFREP 4.1](https://smdg.org/wp-content/uploads/MIGs/TPFREP/TPFREP41_20210525.pdf)). The generous reading therefore puts restows at **2.7–5.7 % of crane lifts**. Colombo is about 80 % transhipment, and multi-port connections should push its rate above a typical gateway port's. That makes it an upper-middle benchmark rather than a low one. The Port of Los Angeles, by contrast, publishes no restow split at all ([Port of Los Angeles](https://portoflosangeles.org/business/statistics/container-statistics/historical-teu-statistics-2024)).

Tariffs show that a restow is billed like a whole extra move, or more. At Adani CMA Mundra a within-bay restow costs **$55 / $82.50 / $110** (20 ft / 40 ft / over 40 ft) and a restow via the quay **$193 / $290 / $386**. A full laden lift of a 20 ft box costs $131, so the via-quay restow costs more than the productive move. Hatch covers cost **$60, or $120 when landed on the quay**, and dangerous-goods boxes carry a 125 % premium ([Adani CMA Mundra tariff](https://www.adaniports.com/-/media/project/ports/portsandterminals/mundra-documents/tariff/ct4_acmtpl-tariff-booklet-wef-01st-june-2025.pdf)). Westports in Port Klang charges **RM 328.90 to land and reship a 20 ft box**, close to the RM 345 for a full import or export move and above the RM 209.30 for a transhipment move. It charges RM 74.80 for a same-bay shift and RM 149.50 to open and close a hatch ([Westports tariff 2025](https://www.westportsholdings.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/2025%20Tariff%20file/Tariff%20Booklet%20-%20Container%20%28Effective%20from%2015.07.2025%29%282%29.pdf?_t=1752829759)). Colombo's SAGT charges **$46.50–80 per ship lift** ([SAGT tariff 2025](https://d3bo42cmohq4cn.cloudfront.net/2025/05/SAGT-Tariff-2025-Final-08.05.2025-1.pdf)). Across these schedules a blended **$50 / $120 / $200 per restowed box** is a reasonable world range. That range is an assumption, because cheap on-board shifts and dear via-quay restows mix in unknown proportions.

Apply Colombo's shares to UNCTAD's **920.3 million TEU** of world port handling ([UNCTAD](https://unctadstat.unctad.org/insights/theme/245)) and the world restows **12.4–26.7 million TEU a year**, or about 7.8–16.7 million boxes. At the blended fees that is a total restow-fee pool of **$0.39 / $1.4 / $3.3 billion a year**. That pool covers every restow, avoidable or not, and no study splits real restows by cause. Dario Pacino's Maersk-backed PhD tested optimised plans on 30 real instances that coordinators had solved, but it did not publish the coordinators' own restow counts ([Pacino thesis, ITU 2012](https://en.itu.dk/-/media/EN/Research/PhD-Programme/PhD-defences/2012/Dario-Pacino-Thesispdf.pdf)). The academic survey of the field separates *mandatory* restows, forced by overstowage, from *voluntary* restows that planners make on purpose to fit more cargo ([van Twiller et al., arXiv 2307.07573](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.07573)). A voluntary restow buys capacity and is not a planning loss. The only figures for the avoidable share come from vendors. Loadmaster.ai claims AI planning cuts restows by 20–40 %, but its attribution to a Springer article could not be checked ([Loadmaster.ai](https://loadmaster.ai/ai-based-restow-minimization-in-deepsea-container-port-vessel-planning/)). Planner on Duty claims up to 30 % fewer restows ([Planner on Duty](https://planneronduty.com/)). Taking 20 / 30 / 40 % as the avoidable share gives **$0.08 / $0.42 / $1.3 billion a year of avoidable restow fees (projection)**, or **$12,000 / $63,000 / $199,000 per ship per year**.

The per-call view makes the scale concrete. Colombo restowed about **24 TEU (roughly 15 boxes) per cargo-ship call** in 2018, and that count covers all restows. Its call count includes non-container ships, so the true figure per container-ship call is somewhat higher. Those boxes cost about **$700–1,200 per call at SAGT on-board rates, or $2,800–4,400 via the quay**. For a feeder of 500–1,000 TEU making 200 calls a year with an assumed 300–600 moves per call, the avoidable restow bill works out to **$10,000–530,000 a year, with a mid of about $90,000**. That overlaps the vendor claim that a typical feeder loses €50,000–150,000 a year to all planning inefficiencies together ([Planner on Duty](https://planneronduty.com/)). Hatch-cover fees are a real gap, because nobody publishes how many hatch-cover lifts a plan causes. As a scaling rule, each avoidable hatch-cover cycle per call worldwide would add **$18–62 million a year** (520,000 calls × $35–120). Shut-out and vessel-change fees exist ($82–164 per box at Mundra), but they are left out. Roll-overs are driven mainly by bookings, and the shut-outs a plan does cause appear in the slot line.

## Trim fuel and lost slots add $0.75 billion at mid, half of it revenue that only moves

The fuel baseline is solid. IMO's data collection system shows container ships of 5,000 GT and above burned **58.96 Mt of fuel in 2023 and 67.49 Mt in 2024**, 28–30 % of all reported ship fuel. The 2024 rise is partly due to Red Sea diversions round the Cape ([IMO MEPC 84/6/1](https://wwwcdn.imo.org/localresources/en/OurWork/Environment/Documents/MEPC%2084-6-1%20-%20Report%20of%20fuel%20oil%20consumption%20data%20submitted%20to%20the%20IMO%20Ship%20Fuel%20Oil%20Consumption%20Databas...%20%28Secretariat%29%5B1%5D.pdf)). Priced at G20 VLSFO annual averages of $535–638/t ([Ship & Bunker](https://shipandbunker.com/news/world/223373-analysis-global-vlsfo-price-sank-by-204-over-course-of-2025)), the fleet's fuel bill is **$31.5 / $39.2 / $43.1 billion a year**, or about **$7.2 million per reporting ship**.

The load plan's share of that bill is not measured. Realised trim-optimisation savings on container ships cluster at **1.5–3 %**. INTERSCHALT reported 2–3 % on a 7,500 TEU ship at 20 knots ([MarineLink](https://www.marinelink.com/amp/news/optimization-improved348251)). Eniram reported 3.1 % confirmed over 500 days on a 5,500 TEU ship, a vendor figure seen only in a snippet ([Business Wire](https://businesswire.com/news/home/20110221005766/en/Eniram-Releases-Extensive-Study-into-Improving-Vessel-Efficiency)). IMO's own range is 0.5–5 %, with partial-load container ships at the top ([IMO GreenVoyage2050](https://greenvoyage2050.imo.org/technology/trim-and-draft-optimization/)). Two statements tie part of this to the plan. IMO notes that little attention is paid to optimal trim and draft when a ship is loaded ([IMO GreenVoyage2050](https://greenvoyage2050.imo.org/technology/trim-and-draft-optimization/)). INTERSCHALT says that after loading the crew has only the ballast tanks left to adjust trim. The plan's share is therefore the part ballast cannot fix, or can fix only by adding weight. Ballast is not always a penalty, though. DNV says large container ships saved 5 % by taking on an extra 10,000 t of ballast to correct trim ([DNV](https://www.dnv.com/news/2014/happy-birthday-eco-assistant-award-winning-trim-optimization-software-enters-5th-year-7908/)). The real cost is the combined trim and ballast penalty against a plan that reaches good trim with less water. An assumed **0.25 / 1.0 / 2.5 %** of fleet fuel gives **$0.08 / $0.39 / $1.08 billion a year (projection)** and **0.46 / 2.09 / 5.23 Mt of CO2**. That range is bounded by the trim studies and by Geissler's simulated trim and ballast savings, which come to roughly 0.75–2.5 % on an assumed voyage burn of about 2,000 t. The line also trades against safety, since BMT warned that trimming for fuel can push lashing forces past their limits ([MarineLink](https://www.marinelink.com/news/containers-steaming388227)).

Lost slot capacity is the least evidenced line. Owners' spec sheets show capacity at 14 t per TEU homogeneous of only **62–66 % of nominal TEU**, a figure seen in snippets ([Lomar spec sheet](https://www.lomarshipping.com/media/2094/shanghai-trader-april-2017.pdf)). On dense trades, then, weight and stability bind before slots run out. That is where planning quality matters. No public source says how often ships sail weight- or stability-limited while cargo is shut out. The only showcase is ONE's MOL Tribute, which carried more than 19,100 TEU on a 20,146 TEU ship using Navis StowMan, with no baseline stated ([AJOT](https://www.ajot.com/news/one-breaks-record-for-highest-utilized-vessel-by-augmenting-human-planners-with-navis-stowage-planning-software)). Carriers realised **about $1,349 per TEU (Maersk 2024, $2,698 per FEU)** and **$1,492 per TEU (Hapag-Lloyd 2024)**, falling to $1,376 in 2025 ([informare](https://new.informare.it/news/gennews/2025/20250187-Maersk-risultati-Y-2024uk.asp); [Hapag-Lloyd](https://www.hapag-lloyd.com/en/company/press/releases/2026/02/hapag-lloyd-publishes-preliminary-business-figures-for-2025.html)). The projection takes 520,000 calls, assumes 2 / 5 / 10 % of departures are affected and 5 / 10 / 20 TEU are recovered on each, and prices them at those rates. It gives **$0.07 / $0.36 / $1.56 billion a year of gross revenue at risk**. Most of a shut-out box sails on the next ship, so this is revenue shifted, not destroyed. The table therefore shows totals with and without it.

The extra crane work the question asks about has no separate line. The restow fee is the market price of those lifts. Pricing them again at Maersk's **$258 total terminal cost per move** would count labour and equipment twice ([Maersk Annual Report 2024](https://www.maersk.com/~/media_sc9/maersk/corporate/sustainability/files/resources/2024/maersk-annual-report-2024.pdf?h=576&hash=39BA5083CD82827B64A382757FDF50A8&w=1024)). Yard rehandles caused by a load sequence that clashes with yard stacking are a real cost. The research notes found no load-plan-attributed yard data, though, so the project's yard line ($0.11 / $0.71 / $3.29 billion) sits outside this evidence and is not added here.

## Berth delay leads at about 100 hours per ship a year, but its high case breaks measured ceilings

The measured port-time data are rich, but none of them isolates the load plan. CPPI 2023 covers 182,855 qualifying calls. It finds an average call of **40.5 hours**, of which **11.7 % was idle at the berth** and **59.4 % was cargo operations**. CPPI estimates that close to four hours per call could be removed through better planning, preparation, communication and streamlined processes ([CPPI 2023](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099060324114539683/pdf/P17583313892300871be641a5ea7b90e0e6.pdf)). That covers every cause. The report concedes that its data cannot identify the root causes of idle time, and it does not reconcile its own figures: 11.7 % of 40.5 h is 4.74 h, not the 3.71 h it quotes. Its gross crane hours include delays caused by the vessel and hatch-cover handling, and its call sizes include restows, so plan-caused time sits inside measured totals and is never broken out. UNCTAD puts the median container-ship call at **0.8 days** at the end of 2024 ([UNCTAD RMT 2025](https://unctad.org/system/files/official-document/rmt2025ch4_en.pdf)). With 520,000 calls spread over 6,642 ships, the average ship makes about **78 calls a year**.

The delay projection reuses the project's assumption that **2 / 5 / 10 % of berth time** disappears with plan-caused work. It applies that to 15 / 26.25 / 45 berth hours per call and prices the time at VHBS 12-month charter assessments of **$17,855 / $39,725 / $72,722 a day** ([VHBS](https://www.vhbs.de/index.php?id=28&L=1)). Only charter time is counted; fuel and downstream cargo costs are left out.

| Case (projection) | Plan-caused berth hours per call | Per ship per year | Per ship per year, USD | World ship-days a year | World, USD billion a year |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Low | 0.30 | 23.5 h (1.0 day) | $17.5k | 6,500 | 0.12 |
| Mid | 1.31 | 103 h (4.3 days) | $170k | 28,400 | 1.13 |
| High, as projected | 4.50 | 352 h (14.7 days) | $1.07M | 97,500 | 7.09 |
| **High, bounded** | **2.63** (10 % of 26.25 h, mid charter) | **205 h (8.6 days)** | **$340k** | **56,900** | **2.26** |

The projected high case fails two measured ceilings. At 4.5 hours a call it exceeds CPPI's entire all-cause removable idle of about 3.7 hours. Its 97,500 ship-days equal about **4 % of all fleet ship-days**. Sea-Intelligence finds that delays from every cause absorbed **2.2 % of fleet capacity in 2011–2019** and **5.3 % in 2023–2026**, the later figure including the Red Sea crisis ([Tradlinx, summarising Sea-Intelligence](https://blogs.tradlinx.com/delays-are-absorbing-1-7m-teu-why-nominal-capacity-is-misleading-shippers/); [Sea-Intelligence](https://sea-intelligence.com/press-room/264-improved-vessel-delays-may-release-more-capacity)). The projected high would blame load plans for most of all liner delay, which is not credible. The bounded high takes 10 % of mid berth time and prices it at the mid charter rate, because about 60 % of CPPI calls are by ships under 5,000 TEU. Even that equals about 70 % of CPPI's all-cause removable idle, so it is a ceiling, not a likely value. The mid case, about 35 % of the all-cause removable idle, gets indirect support from a model study. Planning stowage and crane split together cut berthing time by about **6 %**, a model result read from a listing, not field data ([Chang, Hamedi & Haghani 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/00202940221097981)).

A consistency check with the fees section says where those hours must come from. On the evidence, avoidable restows are 0.3–2.2 % of moves. Spread across the roughly 24 hours of cargo operations in a CPPI call, they account for only **about 0.07–0.5 hours per call**. Most of the mid case's 1.3 hours must therefore come from crane-split imbalance, where the busiest crane (the long crane) sets the departure time, and from late or changed plans. Neither is measured in public data, and terminal delay codes for waiting on a bay plan stay inside operators' terminal operating systems. The study of time as a trade barrier finds each day in transit worth **0.6–2.1 % of cargo value** ([Hummels & Schaur, AER 2013](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:aea:aecrev:v:103:y:2013:i:7:p:2935-59)). That cost is not added here, because schedule buffers absorb most berth hours before they reach the consignee.

## Plan-linked safety losses come to well under one death a year at mid

The measured fire record is grim but small in absolute numbers. CINS logged about **one container cargo fire every 9 days in 2023**, against about one every two weeks in 2020, with lithium-ion batteries now the most common source ([Gard](https://www.gard.no/articles/container-ship-fires-on-the-rise-again/)). Allianz counts a container-ship fire **roughly every 17 days** ([gCaptain](https://gcaptain.com/containership-fire-breaks-out-every-17-days-as-misdeclared-cargo-remains-major-threat/)). EMSA's CARGOSAFE study analysed 124 cargo-fire accidents from 1997–2021 and found the fire rate per ship-year doubled from the early 2000s to the early 2020s. In that study **42 % of cargo fires started in dangerous goods**, 48 % had no established cause, and **27 % of the DG fires came from misdeclared cargo** ([CARGOSAFE Executive Summary](https://emsa.europa.eu/containership-safety/cargosafe/download/7456/4909/23.html); [IACS CCC 10/15/1](https://iacs.s3.af-south-1.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/30162341/CCC-10-15-1-Mitigating-the-risks-and-consequences-of-cargo-fires-on-containerships-IACS.pdf)). Five named cargo-origin fires killed **15 people in 2000–2025, about 0.6 a year, or 0.9 a year over 2015–2025**: Hanjin Pennsylvania 2, MSC Flaminia 3, Maersk Honam 5, Maersk Frankfurt 1 (cause unconfirmed) and Wan Hai 503 4 presumed dead ([Maritime Executive](https://maritime-executive.com/article/survivors-from-wan-hai-503-charged-with-negligence-after-deadly-blast)). CARGOSAFE's historical loss of life per ship-year implies a similar 0.8–1.1 deaths a year. Injuries are badly under-reported. The worst case on record is the KMTC Hong Kong explosion at Laem Chabang in 2019, which sent up to **133 people to hospital** ([FreightWaves](https://www.freightwaves.com/news/boxship-explosion-and-fire-disaster-puts-133-people-in-hospital)). Costs are dominated by a few mega-events. Sri Lanka's Supreme Court ordered about **$1 billion** for X-Press Pearl, against an owner's liability limit of about $25 million ([Maritime Executive](https://maritime-executive.com/article/sri-lanka-orders-1b-in-compensation-for-damage-from-loss-of-x-press-pearl)). MSC Flaminia produced a settlement of up to **$290 million** ([FreightNews](https://www.freightnews.co.za/article/290-million-settlement-reached-msc-flaminia-disaster)). Maersk Honam's general-average and salvage security reached **54 % of cargo value** ([The Loadstar](https://theloadstar.com/shippers-cargo-maersk-honam-must-pay-get-released/)). The all-cause cost of container-ship cargo fires, built from those anchors and from Allianz's finding that fire and explosion make up **18 % of the value of about 244,000 marine claims** ([Allianz](https://commercial.allianz.com/news-and-insights/expert-risk-articles/marine-claims-trends.html)), is roughly **$150 million / $400 million / $1 billion a year**. That figure is itself an assumption-built estimate.

Investigations link the plan to two fires. In both, the stowage was legal for the class the cargo was declared under. On MSC Flaminia, Class 9 DVB was stowed under deck in hold 4, next to heated containers and near heated fuel tanks. The US court nonetheless found the shippers 100 % liable and did not fault the carrier's stowage ([Burgoynes](https://burgoynes.com/articles/2019/02/msc-flaminia-a-brief-account-of-an-investigation)). On Maersk Honam, Singapore's TSIB traced the fire to **54 containers of Class 9 cargo in hold 3**, just forward of the accommodation, and recommended stowing that cargo on deck ([Seatrade](https://www.seatrade-maritime.com/containers/investigation-identifies-series-of-errors-in-maersk-honam-loss)). Maersk then banned IMDG cargo next to the accommodation and engine room ([Maritime Executive](https://maritime-executive.com/article/maersk-overhauls-dangerous-goods-stowage-rules)). CARGOSAFE judged that stowage planning alone has "limited or no effect on reducing the probability of ignition", though it works well alongside detection and firefighting. IACS reports that most large operators already use risk-based stowage beyond the IMDG Code. The KMTC Laem Chabang blast came from undeclared calcium hypochlorite labelled as dolls ([Seatrade](https://www.seatrade-maritime.com/cargo/undeclared-dangerous-cargoes-believed-to-have-caused-fire-explosions-on-kmtc-containership)). That was a screening failure no plan could have caught. The fire projection attributes **2 / 10 / 25 % of costs and injuries** and **5 / 20 / 50 % of deaths** to the plan. Deaths get higher shares because the two stowage-linked fires account for 8 of the 15 deaths. The result is **$3 million / $40 million / $250 million a year and 0.03 / 0.18 / 0.6 deaths a year (projection)**.

Containers lost overboard tell the same story. WSC estimates **1,274 boxes a year over 2015–2024** and **1,478 in 2025**, when MSC Elsa 3 alone lost about 640 ([WSC 2026](https://www.worldshipping.org/news/world-shipping-council-releases-containers-lost-at-sea-report-2026-update)). Catastrophic events made up about 64 % of losses in 2008–2016. The investigations that explicitly blame the plan cover small losses. On YM Efficiency (81 boxes lost), the ATSB found calculated forces exceeded the Cargo Securing Manual (CSM) limits and the stow had not been checked against them in shore planning ([Maritime Executive](https://www.maritime-executive.com/article/atsb-releases-final-report-into-ym-efficiency-container-losses)). On Ever Smart (42 lost), the MAIB found the containers had not been stowed or secured as the Cargo Securing Manual required ([MAIB](https://www.gov.uk/maib-reports/loss-of-cargo-containers-overboard-from-container-ship-ever-smart)). On Annabella, the MAIB found stack weights exceeded while the charterer's planners relied on ship staff to catch errors ([Maritime Executive](https://maritime-executive.com/article/2007-09-27maib-releases-report-on-annabella-acci)). Together these come to only **about 160 boxes in 15 years**, about 1 % of losses. The large losses, such as ONE Apus (1,816 boxes), Maersk Essen (689) and MOL Comfort (about 4,293), are put down to parametric rolling, structural failure and weather. In those reports weights appear only as an uncertainty ([TT Club, on MARIN TopTier](https://www.ttclub.com/news-and-resources/news/article/tt-talk-industry-reports-shed-new-light-on-container-losses-at-sea/)). Wrong input weights remain a live cause. On MSC Napoli, **20 % of the weighed deck boxes were more than 3 t off** their declared weight ([IMO DSC 17/INF.5](https://www.itfglobal.org/sites/default/files/node/resources/files/2012%20DSC%2017-INF.5%20-%20Verification%20of%20container%20weights%20%28Denmark%2C%20Netherlands%2C%20Uni...%29.pdf)). Even after mandatory weighing (VGM) began in 2016, one national dataset still shows **6–8 % of boxes off by more than 1.5 t** ([PortCalls Asia](https://portcalls.com/misdeclaration-of-container-weight-down-since-issuance-of-vgm-rule)). Attributing **5 / 15 / 30 %** of lost boxes to the plan, and adding response, salvage and part of the structural and stability total losses, gives **$6 million / $25 million / $85 million a year (projection)**. No death was reported in any container-ship case with a load-plan finding.

People-harm figures beyond fires rest on the weakest assumptions in the report. ICHCA, via the ITF, counts **more than 700 deaths and 200 serious injuries in port cargo handling from 2000 to 2022**, rising from 18 deaths in 2000 to **87 in 2022**, across all cargo types ([ITF](https://www.itfseafarers.org/sites/default/files/node/resources/files/A5%20-%20Cargo%20Handling_0.pdf)). ICHCA's own 2025 dashboard gives a lower count of more than 500 for 2000 to mid-2025 ([ICHCA](https://ichca.com/ichca-severe-risks-dashboard-june-2025)). No investigation ties a lashing death to what the plan contained. Assuming a 30 % container share and a 0 / 5 / 15 % plan link gives 0 / 0.5 / 4 deaths a year. The high case adds about one death a year to allow that a deadly stability capsize could be plan-related. Combined, plan-attributable safety losses come to **$9 million / $65 million / $335 million a year, 0.03 / 0.7 / 5.6 deaths and about 0.1 / 0.7 / 4 reported injuries (projection)**. No statistical value of life is added, because none was sourced.

## The project's 5/10/15 % restow share runs about twelve times too high

The evidence shows plainly that [`docs/impact.md`](../docs/impact.md) overstates restows by roughly an order of magnitude, on three counts. First, the project treats 5 / 10 / 15 % of all ship moves as *extra*, avoidable restows that a better plan removes. But the only measured port-wide rate for *all* restows is **1.35–2.9 % of TEU handled**, and still only **2.7–5.7 % of lifts** on the reading that counts two lifts per restow ([SLPA](https://www.parliament.lk/uploads/documents/paperspresented/annual-report-srilanka-ports-authority-2018.pdf)). The project's *low* case for avoidable restows already equals the top of the measured range for total restows, at a hub where restows should run above average. Second, only part of any restow total is avoidable. Voluntary restows buy capacity, and others follow from the rotation, late load-list changes and roll-overs. The 20–40 % avoidable band is itself a vendor claim. Third, the figures at 15 % and above do not hold up. Loadmaster.ai's claim that restows reach 15–20 % of moves cites an Erasmus MSc thesis that never mentions restows ([Loadmaster.ai](https://loadmaster.ai/balancing-restow-reduction-and-crane-productivity-in-deepsea-container-ports/); [EUR thesis](https://thesis.eur.nl/pub/60416/Okeke-Adaeze-Montserrat.pdf)). DocShipper's 15–25 % for poor stowage gives no data ([DocShipper](https://docshipper.com/glossary/block-stowage-definition-logistics/)). Planner on Duty's calculator defaults to an 18 % restow ratio and gives no source. The 17–28 % from the project's own Jensen and Ajspur citation is work *required* on a constructed stress voyage even when moves are minimised. It is not waste ([Sealytix](https://www.sealytix.com/media/gm4flxf5/revenuemanagementinlinershippingaddressingthevesselcapacitychallenge.pdf)).

The price per lift is not the problem. The project's $30 / $75 / $150 per extra lift fits the tariffs above. The problem is the share. Evidence-based inputs are total restows of **1.5 / 3 / 5.5 % of moves** (Colombo, with the two-lift reading and an allowance for hatch covers and on-board shifts) times the vendor-derived **20 / 30 / 40 %** avoidable share. Together they give **0.3 / 0.9 / 2.2 % avoidable**. Put through the project's own formula and prices, that changes the numbers as follows.

| Ship-restow line in `docs/impact.md` | Low | Mid | High |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Project share of moves (extra restows) | 5 % | 10 % | 15 % |
| Evidence-based avoidable share (projection) | 0.3 % | 0.9 % | 2.2 % |
| Project extra lifts, millions a year | 32.3 | 68.2 | 108.3 |
| Evidence-based extra lifts, millions a year | 1.8 | 5.6 | 13.8 |
| Overstatement | about 17× | about 12× | about 8× |
| Project restow line, USD billion | 0.97 | 5.11 | 16.24 |
| Evidence-based restow line, USD billion | 0.06 | 0.42 | 2.07 |
| Project total pool, USD billion | 1.2 | 7.0 | 26.7 |
| **Pool with only the restow share corrected, USD billion** | **0.31** | **2.3** | **12.5** |

The restow line is **73 % of the project's $7.0 billion mid pool**, so the headline rests mostly on the input the evidence contradicts. Correcting that share alone takes the mid to **about $2.3 billion**. Bounding the berth-time high as described above also takes the high from $26.7 billion to **about $7.7 billion**. The input of 10 % restows at $75 printed on the pitch's on-screen card needs to change to match. On the same call-share scaling the project uses, the Laem Chabang figure falls from about $125 million to **about $41 million a year (projection)**. One counter-argument should be weighed. Colombo is one port, it may exclude on-board shifts and hatch-cover lifts, and short-sea feeder loops with eight ports per rotation could restow more. No public data shows any of that, and a mid case of 10 % avoidable would need such ports to restow at roughly two to seven times Colombo's measured rate, depending on how restows are counted, with every restow avoidable.

## Conclusion

The correction shifts the case for a better load planner away from restow fees. On the evidence, avoidable restows are a sub-1 % slice of crane work. They can explain only a fraction of an hour per call, so the plan's largest economic lever is berth time. That time is lost to an overloaded long crane and to plans that arrive late or change, and those are exactly the mechanisms public data does not measure. The project's most defensible measured contribution is therefore not a world dollar figure. It is a simulated, clearly labelled before-and-after comparison of crane hours and restow counts on its own ships. Better public evidence would need terminal delay codes, cause-coded restows from a carrier, or a second port authority's restow table, such as SLPA's 2023 report or Trinidad's monthly series.

On safety, the honest claim is narrow. It is not about volume, and it is not fire prevention. Investigators in Australia, the UK and Singapore keep finding plans that broke Cargo Securing Manual stack, tier and lashing limits, or stowed declared dangerous goods legally but badly next to heat or crew, with no one checking before sailing. A planner gated by a checker, that keeps oxidisers on deck and away from the accommodation and engine room and treats declared weights as uncertain, addresses that documented failure. Its benefit shows up as less severe incidents, not fewer of them, and it will be largest for feeders and smaller lines that lack the risk-zone software of the big carriers.
