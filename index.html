StowLM

The AI pipeline around a ship-planning engine, as an app. Messy voyage documents and a planner's own words go in, a checked plan and a handover package come out, with a fine-tuned Meta model on the laptop reading every instruction.

Models propose. Software decides.

A forward pilot, built to scale

A feeder ship's loading plan starts from paper: a tally sheet, an email that contradicts it, a limit called over a bad radio line. StowLM is the AI pipeline that turns that mess into a plan the full checker passes and a handover package for the ship's master, delivered as an app. Muse Spark reads the cargo papers, Muse Voice Transcribe hears the planner, a fine-tuned Muse Glimmer 30B reads the planner's own words into rules on the laptop, the planner confirms each reading, the engine plans the section, and a checker with 54 active rules decides. The engine sits in one slot: ours today, and by design a firm's own planning software. Today's pilot runs the loop on synthetic ships inside a stated design limit. It is a forward pilot, built to scale: more compute for the search, a larger model for reading.

How it fits together

One slot for the engine

Design and potential: only Engine 0.13.0 is wired in; no third-party engine has been ported

Everything around the engine is the pipeline: the paper intake, the instruction readers, the planner's confirmation, the full checker, the rule-tier ladder, the signed handover and both apps. Frontier Engine 0.13.0, our search software, is the reference engine in the slot. The design lets a carrier, terminal or stowage-software vendor put its own planning software there and keep everything around it.

What the slot is today. The runner reaches the engine through one binding (execute_brief and plan_with_rules: the instance and the planner's confirmed rules in, a plan in the pipeline's own format out). The runner then checks that plan itself on the original instance, with the full checker and every confirmed instruction, before anything is drawn or packaged, and the binding pins the engine's source manifest. In the held-out evaluation the simple two-step planner's plans went through the same checker and tier classification, so the gate already judges two different planners; that ran in the evaluation harness, not through the app's slot.

What a port would take. The firm's software behind those two functions, its own source pin, and its own way to honour the confirmed rules (today the binding applies them inside Engine 0.13.0). Every result on this page was measured with Engine 0.13.0 in the slot and says nothing about another engine.

Three Meta models, each with one job

On the laptop · fine-tuned by us

Muse Glimmer 30B, as StowLM Reader

Fine-tuned with LoRA. Reads the planner's words into rules, on device. It reads free wording of four kinds of instruction and is built to refuse anything else; it can slip, so the planner confirms.

118 of 128 (92.19 %) on a locked reader test, against 60 of 128 (46.88 %) for the untrained Glimmer under the same mask: same model, same laptop, with and without our fine-tune.

Named comparison: Muse Spark read 126 of 128 (fixed reference, earlier mask, not rerun). The reader cleared the 90 % gate and missed two stricter ones: it accepted one loosening where none was allowed, and it sits 6 points below Spark where 3 were allowed.

New wording from another model (100 synthetic sentences, one pre-registered run, not real planner text): 86 of 100 read exactly, against 45 without our fine-tune; 61 of 66 rules, against 18. It also made a rule from 6 of 34 sentences it should have refused, so a person confirms every reading. It read 4 of 5 Thai-English sentences.

Why it matters, if it scales

Who it's for: the planners who load feeder ships at ports like Laem Chabang, where a load plan starts from a tally sheet, an email and a radio call; and, through the engine slot, carriers, terminals and stowage-software vendors who could keep their own planning software and add the pipeline around it (potential; nothing ported yet). A forward pilot, built to scale: more compute for the search, a larger model for reading.

Projection, not a result. None of it has been measured.

Bad load plans cost about $0.35 to $6.6 billion a year worldwide, mid about $2.4 billion, across restow fees, fuel, lost slots, delay and safety, and 0.03 to 5.6 deaths a year (mid 0.7) (projection; docs/load-plan-losses.md). The figures below are the narrower part a better plan acts on directly.

about $90 billion a year

of port-call cost worldwide, so every 1 % is about $1 billion. Projection: exposure

about $2.3 billion a year

of extra crane work and berth time that a better plan can act on (mid case; low $0.3 billion). Projection: pool

$63–76 billion a year

Check: world terminal revenue, analyst estimates. The mid-case restow line ($0.42 billion) sits inside it, not on top of it.

About $40 million a year at Laem Chabang if scaled by its share of calls (projection; 9,325 of about 520,000 calls, 1.8 %). Half the pool is lifts, which scale with TEU, and the port's calls skew to feeders, so this is a share of calls, not a port estimate.

Inputs

  • 920M TEU handled a year (UNCTAD, 2024)
  • 520k container-ship calls a year (UNCTAD, rounded)
  • assumed 0.9 % avoidable restows, at $75 a lift
  • assumed 5 % less berth time if the extra work goes

What would have to be true

  • full-ship, multi-port planning works;
  • these rates hold worldwide;
  • the extra work is avoidable;
  • terms that permit operational use: today's Muse Glimmer usage policy prohibits use related to operating transportation technologies where harm is possible, so a deployment would need Meta's permission or a base model whose terms allow it, plus class approval;
  • none of it has been measured.

Today's pilot runs the loop on synthetic half-ship sections of 500–800 TEU feeders. The path is more compute for the search and a larger model for reading, toward 1,700, 8,000 and 20,000 TEU ships (projection). The engine isn't competitive at large scale yet. This is a gross opportunity scenario, not a saving achieved.

What the planner hands the master

Stored plan, computed on 2 Nov 2026, checked on 2 Nov 2026 · synthetic ships and cargo, simulated port · our own signature, not a certificate

The bay-nine hero: one sentence over the radio, "Nothing goes into bay nine on this call.", read on the laptop by StowLM Reader, then planned by Engine 0.13.0 and passed by the full checker. The handover package that comes out holds:

  • Bay plans, one printed A3 page per planned bay: 87 boxes in 6 of 12 bays of a half-ship section, beside 168 fixed synthetic background boxes.
  • The crane sequence and yard sequence, as CSV files.
  • A note to the master with the stability figures, dangerous-goods and reefer slots, the tightest limits, and the confirmed instruction.
  • A signed receipt tying each step to its model, hash and checker verdict. The signed package names the frozen pipeline it came from (v4, acf2f275…). It's our own signature, not a certificate.

The instruction is printed in the note to the master: "Confirmed instruction: Nothing goes into bay nine on this call. Confirmed by: user-authorised batch regeneration, no planner present (demo fixture)." The user authorised the batch rebuild; no planner confirmed it live.
