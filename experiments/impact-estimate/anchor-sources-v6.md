## Real 500–800 TEU regional activity: useful evidence, no eligible-call count

[Regional Container Lines, service launch announcement, **2020-11-23**](https://www.rclgroup.com/PressReleaseArticle/NEWS1222) announced a **weekly** Singapore–Cebu–Cagayan de Oro–Singapore service using **two** ships, Ratha Bhum and Pira Bhum. [RCL's own Annual Report **2020**, fleet-profile table](https://www.rclgroup.com/pdf/AnnualReports/2020_AnnualReport_ENG_V2.pdf) lists each ship at **628 TEU designed capacity**. Together these are direct carrier evidence that ships within the pilot's vessel-size range have operated regional feeder services. They are historical evidence, not proof of today's ships or sailings. They do not establish empty arrival, fill or loading scope. If annualising weekly frequency, **52 calls per named port per assumed 52-week year** is a computed nominal schedule opportunity, conditional on uninterrupted operation; it is not observed realised annual calls.

A separate direct carrier example is [RCL's **2019-03-04** RCH8 announcement](https://rclgroup.com/PressReleaseArticle/NEWS1187): Port Klang–Calcutta–Port Klang with **628 and 750 TEU** vessels and an **18-day rotation**. This is a historical SEA-connected service, not a Thai call count. It can support regional relevance but must not inflate the addressable denominator.

The live [CNC RBH flyer](https://www.cnc-line.com/ebusiness/schedules/line-services/flyer/RBHCNC), checked **2026-11-01**, publishes **weekly** frequency, **two** vessels and a **14-day** rotation, with Laem Chabang on northbound and southbound legs. ## What the workflow changes; what to measure

The intended pilot reduces the work between messy voyage documents and a checked plan package: reconcile rows and yard state, keep an instruction attached to its confirmed reading, turn changes into checked proposals, and retain the reasoning and checks in handover. Instruction-change turnaround in minutes is a **pilot target / projection** until task 3.1 supplies final-pipeline measurements. Suggested placeholders: `{{packet_to_checked_plan_median_minutes}}`, `{{instruction_change_to_checked_plan_median_minutes}}`, `{{sealed_envelope_passes}}`, `{{sealed_envelope_maps}}`, `{{intake_row_accuracy}}`, `{{reader_sealed_accuracy}}`. They are not baseline evidence.

For workflow stakes, the [UK MAIB Annabella investigation, Report **21/2007**, published **September 2007**, synopsis printed pp.1–2](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/547c7032e5274a429000007d/AnnabellaReport.pdf) identifies weaknesses in information flow among shippers, planners, terminal and vessel, and the difficulty of controlling a final loading plan under operational pace. It recommends effective communication and enough review time. This supports keeping instructions and checks together; it gives no rate of rework, lost instructions, prevented incidents or value for StowLM.

No numerical baseline was verified for local document-cleaning hours, frequency of instruction changes, rework probability, approval wait, or planner wage. Keep those as explicit assumptions, or leave them unmonetised. A prospective pilot should record active planner minutes before and after (including review/corrections), total turnaround time separately, instruction retention, invalid intake/refusal rates, signed extra-lift differences including fallback/repair work, and actual approval time. Count a repeated task only once: planner attention saved and rework attention saved can overlap. Machine runtime is neither attention time nor berth time.

### Actual terminal cost-per-move evidence

[Maersk Annual Report **2024**, Terminals, printed pp.36–37](https://www.maersk.com/~/media_sc9/maersk/corporate/sustainability/files/resources/2024/maersk-annual-report-2024.pdf?h=576&hash=39BA5083CD82827B64A382757FDF50A8&w=1024) reports **USD 258 terminal cost per move**, financially consolidated, versus **USD 252 in 2023**; revenue per move was **USD 337**. The report attributes changes partly to depreciation, mix and utilization. This is broad terminal-segment accounting across gateways, including fixed/overhead and capital-related costs; it is **not the marginal cost of an extra yard lift**, not a tariff, and not a number to multiply by every avoided rehandle. It demonstrates why current world terminal economics cannot be represented by one historical small-container tariff. It also warns against counting the entire terminal workflow cost as one crane cycle. No independently verified worldwide marginal extra-lift cost distribution was found.

### Recommended price treatment

For the world scenario, **USD 30 / 75 / 150 per avoided extra lift are defensible labelled price-proxy assumptions**, not sourced percentiles, an average, a cost estimate, or confidence bounds. The directly readable USD per-lift charge anchors range from Chennai **20.86** through Colombo **46.50–80** to Los Angeles **205.945**; Liverpool is higher-valued sterling evidence, left unconverted. The three rounded choices lie inside that broad observed public-charge span. Their application to a global **mix of yard and quay** lifts is still an explicit scenario assumption; a valid operational appraisal needs category-specific local marginal costs and actual work eliminated. Prefer separate yard and quay prices when the model has their shares. No researched portfolio weights justify calling the three choices a weighted world price. Do not sum public charges with terminal costs or multiply both by the same avoided lift.

## Original-paper yard rehandle and ship-restow evidence

**Best directly available yard-ratio source:** [Legato and Mazza, *Managing Container Reshuffling in Vessel Loading by Simulation*, Winter Simulation Conference **2013**, Table 2 printed p.3460 (PDF p.11)](https://informs-sim.org/wsc13papers/includes/files/305.pdf). It uses **real Medcenter/Gioia Tauro loading plans** but **simulated rehandle counts**, not observed saved moves. With one straddle carrier per quay crane, the loading plans contain **84 / 360 / 633 containers** and rehandle counts or interval estimates are **18 / 32.64–33.24 / 23.67–24.05**. Computed extra yard rehandles per productive loading move are **21.43% / 9.07–9.23% / 3.74–3.80%**. These are three particular configurations, not feeder/large-ship universal rates. The rules-of-thumb upper bounds in the same table are **18 / 86 / 209**; using those as observed counts would materially exaggerate the larger examples. PDF extracted directly during this research.

**Original-paper improvement context:** [Ries, González-Ramírez and Miranda, ICORES **2017**, container dwell-time/stacking study](https://www.scitepress.org/papers/2017/61930/61930.pdf), discusses **16,867** baseline rehandles for **January–April 2012**, compared with **14,051** under its proposed segregated sequential strategy, approximately **17% fewer rehandles**. That percentage's denominator is **baseline rehandles**, not primary loads or all crane moves. Its **USD 10 per rehandle** is an estimated cost assumption, not a measured tariff or universal cost. This supports optimization opportunity, not transferring its effect to StowLM or using 17% as the global extra-lift ratio.

**Required restows in a stress voyage — context only:** [Jensen and Ajspur, *Revenue management in liner shipping: Addressing the vessel capacity challenge*, Maritime Transport Research **3 (2022), 100069**, §7.2.1, Table 10](https://www.sealytix.com/media/gm4flxf5/revenuemanagementinlinershippingaddressingthevesselcapacitychallenge.pdf). A **constructed-voyage** experiment has productive baseline **8,977 crane moves**; two cases total **12,531.0 / 10,810.7**, with **3,554.0 / 1,833.7 extra restow moves**. Published rounded shares of **all** moves are **28.4% / 17.0%**. For study-denominator comparison only, the original counts give: **3,554/8,977 = 39.5901%** and **1,833.7/8,977 = 20.4266%**, not 28.4% and 17.0%. The objective minimizes these required moves under fixed stress-voyage cargo conditions. Neither percentage calibrates the calculator or measures avoidable waste, an observed world rate, or a StowLM effect. See `scale-sources-v6.md` for context.

**Historical observed yard-rate lead, not primary-verified here:** Chen, Lin and Juang (**2000**), DOI [10.1080/030888300286608](https://doi.org/10.1080/030888300286608), is a genuine empirical study, but only its abstract was publicly retrievable. The **9% / 17%** loaded-container shift rates and **21%** discharged-container housekeeping rate quoted in the [Massey thesis](https://mro.massey.ac.nz/bitstreams/1b75d683-06dc-4daf-92d5-e660f12c5dd4/download) remain **secondary-reported, not directly checked against the original paper**. Do not label them directly sourced empirical calibration. The Legato original paper above is a cleaner public source for explicit modelled ratio context. No primary observed global ship-restow share was verified. Yard and ship ratios have different denominators and scopes; do not add them without a population/work-category split.
